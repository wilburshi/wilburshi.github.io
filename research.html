Research in depth

How does the brain compute strategic social decisions?

My research program is dedicated to understanding how neural circuits compute adaptive, strategic decisions — in both individual and social contexts. My training across two complementary model systems has given me a principled framework for linking behavior, computation, and circuit-level mechanisms. My long-term goal is to build a mechanistic model of how the cortico-striatal circuit generates prosocial decisions, with translational relevance to neuropsychiatric disorders and to the development of social agents in AI.

PhD work · Washington University in St. Louis

Causal function of orbitofrontal cortex in economic decision-making

How does value get computed in the brain, and how does that computation drive choice? My doctoral work in Dr. Camillo Padoa-Schioppa's lab addressed these questions using microstimulation and single-unit electrophysiology in macaques performing economic choice tasks.

OFC activity is causally related to economic choices

A central question in decision neuroscience is whether neural activity in a given region merely correlates with behavior, or actually drives it. Using microstimulation at varying current levels, we showed that OFC activity is causally linked to economic choices: low-current stimulation facilitated value encoding and biased choices toward the stimulated option, while high-current stimulation disrupted value representations and impaired decision-making. Notably, even very low currents could disrupt choices — but specifically at the value comparison stage rather than the valuation stage itself, suggesting that distinct current levels engage different computational processes and possibly different cell types within the OFC.

A stable OFC circuit supports both simultaneous and sequential offers

Economic decisions can unfold in different formats — offers may be presented simultaneously or one at a time. We asked whether the OFC circuit adapts to these different task structures or whether a single underlying circuit supports both. By randomly interleaving simultaneous and sequential trials, we identified the same functional cell populations in both conditions, demonstrating that the OFC operates as a single, stable decision circuit across diverse economic contexts.

Neural origins of decision variability

Choices are not perfectly consistent — they vary across trials and conditions in systematic ways. Using the two-task paradigm, we dissected three distinct sources of variability under sequential offers: decision noise (noisier choices), order bias (favoring the second offer), and preference bias (amplified existing preferences). Each phenomenon mapped onto a distinct neural process: noisier decisions correlated with weaker valuation signals, while both order and preference biases correlated with the value comparison process. This decomposition clarified how OFC valuation and comparison mechanisms each contribute differently to behavioral variability.

Postdoctoral work · Yale University

Neural mechanisms of cooperative social decisions in freely moving animals

As a Wu Tsai Postdoctoral Fellow at Yale, working with Drs. Steve Chang, Anirvan Nandy, and Monika Jadi, I extended my decision neuroscience framework to cooperative social behavior. A key challenge in social neuroscience is that most paradigms are artificial and constrained. My work has focused on developing tools and paradigms that allow naturalistic, freely-moving social interactions to be studied at neural resolution.

An integrated platform for naturalistic social neuroscience

I co-developed a platform combining a cooperative lever-pulling apparatus, deep-learning behavioral tracking (DeepLabCut + Anipose for 3D reconstruction), and a custom wireless high-density neural recording system. This enables simultaneous measurement of behavior and neural activity in freely interacting dyads — a methodological advance that makes the subsequent scientific questions tractable.

Diverse strategies enable successful cooperation

Using Bayesian models to characterize the moment-to-moment dynamics of cooperative interactions, we found that dyads do not converge on a single fixed strategy. Instead, they develop diverse and flexible behavioral repertoires — and this flexibility is itself a key predictor of cooperative success. This work established a quantitative framework for characterizing social strategy at the individual level.

Gaze-dependent evidence accumulation in dmPFC underlies cooperative decisions

In a first-author study combining neural recordings with drift-diffusion modeling, I showed that social gaze acts as a gating mechanism for evidence accumulation: animals sample their partner's behavior through directed gaze, and this information is integrated in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) to drive cooperative decisions. Neural population dynamics in dmPFC track the accumulation process in real time, linking a specific computational framework to its circuit substrate. This work establishes the dmPFC as a key node for social evidence integration during naturalistic cooperation.

OFC as a goal-directed cognitive map for social and non-social behavior

Bridging my PhD and postdoctoral work, we proposed that the OFC functions as a goal-directed cognitive map — integrating task-relevant information to guide flexible behavior across both social and non-social contexts. This framework predicts that OFC neural computations in social settings should reflect the integration of social information (partner state, social history) alongside non-social variables (reward, cost), producing the diverse strategies observed behaviorally.

Future directions · Independent research program

Cortico-striatal circuit mechanisms of strategic social decisions

My independent research program will build a circuit-level, mechanistic understanding of how the brain navigates strategic social interactions under conditions of inequity — when costs and rewards are unevenly distributed between agents. The central hypothesis is that communication between the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) and the dorsal striatum (DS) implements the computations that guide these choices.

Aim 1

A model-free approach to discover cooperative strategies and their neural correlates in dmPFC under effort inequity. Using a novel Effort-Inequity Cooperation task with systematic variation of self- and partner-cost, combined with high-density wireless recordings and Dynamic Bayesian Network analysis, we will characterize the behavioral strategy space and its dmPFC representation.

Aim 2

A model-based approach to dissect the cognitive mechanisms of strategic decisions. Building on the DDM framework from my postdoc, I will test how effort costs modulate specific decision parameters — self-cost biasing the starting point, partner-cost modulating drift rate — and link these model parameters directly to dmPFC population dynamics. A complementary reinforcement learning framework will address how strategies are learned and updated over time.

Aim 3

Circuit-level investigation of cortico-striatal dynamics. Using simultaneous multi-region wireless recordings from dmPFC and dorsal striatum, we will test whether dmPFC encodes high-level strategic variables while the striatum translates these into action policies. A Multi-Agent Recurrent Neural Network (MARNN) model with separate dmPFC and DS modules will synthesize findings into a generalized computational framework.

In the longer term, my lab will pursue causal interventions (optogenetics, wireless microstimulation, focused ultrasound) to move beyond correlation to circuit-level causation; expand to more complex game-theoretic paradigms and group social settings; and use the principles uncovered to inform the development of social agents in AI that can navigate inequity and cooperate strategically.
